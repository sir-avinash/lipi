# Engaging exposition

A chapter is absorbing when the reader always has an open question they care about and a fair chance of answering it. Everything below serves that.

## The shape of a chapter

1. **Open with a puzzle the reader thinks is easy.** One concrete question with a wrong obvious answer. No roadmap, no learning objectives, no thesis statement. The puzzle is the roadmap.
   > Your robot's heading is 350°. You want it at 10°. What is the error?
2. **Concrete before general.** One dimension before three. A number before a formula. A picture before a definition. State the theorem after the reader could have guessed it.
3. **Let thought experiments carry the hard ideas.** Something the reader can do in their head or with their hands: nested rings, a plate on a palm, two labs with differently mounted cameras. Name each one. A good thought experiment makes the abstract statement feel like a summary.
4. **Give each definition a job before it arrives.** Show the failure it prevents or the question it answers, then define it.
5. **Read every formula aloud once.** After a display, say what each piece does in plain words. "A height, zero at the target, rising to 2 at half a turn away."
6. **End each section with a reason to read the next.** A question, an unexplained piece, a failure of what was just built.
7. **Be honest about limits.** Say what a result does not give. Readers trust a text that tells them where it stops working.
8. **Close with work, then sources.** Exercises that ask for a derivation, an experiment or a counterexample. Then two or three sentences on who did what, and a reference list.

## Sentences

- Short. One idea each. Split any sentence with three clauses.
- No em-dashes. Use a full stop, a colon or parentheses.
- Plain verbs and nouns. Cut "leverage", "delve", "crucial", "robust framework", "it is worth noting that", "in this chapter we will".
- Do not announce. Say the thing.
- "You" for the reader. "We" for shared steps of a derivation.
- Define a term once, in bold, at first use. Never rename it.
- Vary rhythm. A three-word sentence after two long ones lands.
- Prefer a specific number to an adjective. "2300° per second" beats "extremely fast".

## Tests for a draft

- **The cold-open test.** Delete the first paragraph. If nothing is lost, it was throat-clearing.
- **The bullet test.** If a passage could be a bullet list without loss, it is not yet an explanation. Explanations have "because" in them.
- **The skim test.** Read only the first sentence of each paragraph. The argument should survive.
- **The stranger test.** Every symbol is defined before use, in the chapter or by an explicit reference.
- **The checked test.** Every number and every identity in the text is produced by something that runs.
- **The cut test.** Remove any sentence that neither helps the reader understand nor helps them do something.

## What to avoid

- "Core thesis" boxes, learning-objective lists and summaries of what is about to be said.
- Runs of definitions with no example between them.
- Adjectives doing the work of evidence: "elegant", "powerful", "fundamental".
- Claims of global or optimal behaviour stated more strongly than the theorem.
- Citations you cannot identify precisely. Quotes you cannot source.
- Any mention of how the text was produced.

## Rewriting an existing draft

1. List the claims and topics. Treat the draft as an inventory, not as text.
2. Check every claim. Drop or fix what fails, and record it.
3. Find the puzzle. Often it is buried in the draft's third section as an "application".
4. Re-order concrete to general.
5. Write fresh. Do not edit sentence by sentence: the old rhythm survives.
6. Apply the tests above.
